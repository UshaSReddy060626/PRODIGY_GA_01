The old lighthouse keeper climbed the spiral stairs every evening before sunset. He had done this for thirty years, and the rhythm of it felt as natural as breathing. From the top, he could see the fishing boats returning to harbor, their lanterns flickering like scattered stars on the dark water.

The village below was small, no more than a few dozen houses clustered around a stone church. Most of the people who lived there had never left, content with the slow turning of the seasons and the familiar faces at the market on Saturdays.

In winter, storms would batter the coastline for days at a time. The lighthouse keeper would stay awake through the worst of them, watching the beam sweep across the churning sea, hoping every ship out there could see it in time.

His granddaughter visited every summer. She loved climbing the stairs with him, counting each step out loud, always losing count somewhere around fifty. She would ask him questions about the sea, about the ships, about what it felt like to be so far from everything and so close to it at the same time.

He never had a good answer. The sea was simply there, the way the sky was there, the way the wind was there. It did not need explaining. It only needed watching.

Years later, after the lighthouse was automated and he no longer needed to climb the stairs each evening, he would still walk down to the shore at sunset. Old habits, he told himself, though it was more than that. It was the only place he still felt entirely himself.

The town eventually built a small museum near the lighthouse, filled with old maps, ship logs, and photographs of the fishing fleet from decades past. Visitors would stop by on their way to the beach, curious about the tower that had watched over the coast for so long.

Every autumn, the wind would shift, carrying the scent of salt and rain further inland than usual. Fishermen said it meant an early winter. The old keeper always believed them, though he could never say exactly why he trusted the wind's opinion on such matters.

Some nights, when the fog rolled in thick and low, the foghorn would sound every thirty seconds, a deep mournful note carried across the water. It was, in its own way, a kind of lullaby for the whole village, one that meant home was still there, still watching, still waiting for everyone to come back.
